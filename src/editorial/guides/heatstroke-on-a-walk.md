A dog that slows down, pants heavily, seems unsteady or collapses on a hot walk needs action. Exercise itself generates heat. A shaded route and an ordinary morning temperature do not rule out heat-related illness. Flat-faced dogs, older dogs and animals with breathing difficulties may struggle sooner.

## Start at the scene
Stop exercise, move into shade or a ventilated place, and begin cooling with water cooler than the dog. Get water onto the body through the coat while keeping the head and airway clear. Use moving air if available. Call the nearest veterinary service on speaker or have another person call. Follow its directions on the best cooling method for the individual dog. The Royal Veterinary College advises starting cooling before transport.

A wet towel draped over the dog can trap heat as it warms. Pouring water over the body and allowing airflow is more useful. Do not force water into the mouth of a distressed, weak or unconscious dog. Tell the clinic if the dog is vomiting, disoriented or unable to stand.

## Hand over the timeline
Report the activity, approximate time signs began, any period of collapse and what cooling you have already done. Use ventilation in the vehicle and keep observation possible. Do not cancel the visit because panting eases. Heat injury can affect internal organs after the dog appears more comfortable.

## Change the next outing
Use the dog’s response as well as the weather forecast. Choose shorter routes with shade and an easy exit, take drinking water and let the dog set the pace. A ball game with repeated sprints may create more heat than a gentle walk of the same duration. Ask the vet whether an underlying airway or health problem changes future exercise advice.

<details class="story-faq">
<summary>Does a dog have to be in a parked car to get heatstroke?</summary>
<p>No. Heat production during exercise can exceed a dog’s ability to lose heat, particularly with humidity, strenuous play or impaired breathing. If several dogs usually walk together, watch each individual. A younger companion still pulling on the lead is no guide to what an older dog can tolerate.</p>
</details>
