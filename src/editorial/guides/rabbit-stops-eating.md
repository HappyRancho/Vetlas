A rabbit that refuses breakfast and produces fewer or smaller droppings needs prompt veterinary attention. Do not wait until the next morning to see whether a favourite treat works. Pain, dental problems and other illnesses can slow the gut, and an obstruction can look similar at home. “Stasis” describes what the gut is doing, not the underlying diagnosis.

## Make the call while someone prepares the carrier
Ask whether a rabbit-experienced clinician can assess your rabbit now. Give the last time you actually saw hay eaten, the last normal droppings, and whether the rabbit is quiet, hunched, grinding its teeth or distended. If the first practice cannot receive rabbits, ask where to go immediately. A quieter rabbit is not necessarily a more comfortable rabbit.

## Separate observation from a guess
In a shared enclosure, an empty bowl tells you very little about which rabbit ate. Say so plainly. Photograph the litter area or take a small sample of recent droppings if this takes no extra time. Mention new foods, access to an object that might have been chewed, recent medicines and any previous dental treatment.

## What is safe while arranging transport?
Offer familiar hay and water to an alert rabbit that chooses to eat. Keep transport quiet and secure. Do not force-feed, give a gut stimulant or use leftover medication before a vet has assessed the possibility of obstruction. A sick rabbit can aspirate material given by mouth. Follow the receiving clinic’s instructions.

When the rabbit is discharged, ask what quantity and appearance of droppings to expect, how to monitor eating in a bonded pair and which change requires an immediate return. Record the recheck appointment before leaving. Fewer pellets on the floor and fewer droppings in the tray are different observations.

<details class="story-faq">
<summary>Is accepting a treat reassuring?</summary>
<p>It proves that the rabbit took that item. It does not establish normal hay intake or gut movement. Report selective eating too, particularly if your rabbit approaches hay then drops it or seems unable to chew. Mouth pain and abdominal illness can both change which foods disappear first.</p>
</details>
