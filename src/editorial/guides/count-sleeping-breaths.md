If your vet asks for a sleeping respiratory rate, count when your dog or cat is genuinely asleep and comfortable. The purpose is to compare like with like. A dog panting after a walk, a cat purring in your lap or a pet twitching through a dream is a poor baseline. Laboured breathing while awake needs attention in its own right.

## Find the movement and time it
Watch from the side without disturbing your pet. One rise and fall of the chest is one breath. Count for a full minute, or count for 30 seconds and double the result. For example, 11 rises in half a minute is 22 breaths per minute. If you lose track or the animal wakes up, record that and try later when comfortable. Do not delay care to obtain a perfect number.

## Give the number context
Keep the original count, duration, whether the animal was asleep, and any cough or effort. A note such as “11 in 30 seconds, asleep on side, no effort” is much easier to interpret than “breathing okay.” In stable animals a sleeping rate is commonly below 30 per minute. Ask your team for your animal’s usual range and its specific call threshold. Report a sustained increase even when a single measurement looks close to that range.

## When should I put the timer down?
Open-mouth breathing in a cat, obvious abdominal effort, an inability to settle, pale or blue gums, or collapse calls for urgent assessment. Keep handling minimal, call ahead and travel. Do not add a dose of a heart medicine unless the treating vet has given that exact instruction. A video taken safely by someone else can help the team, but the number and video are both secondary to getting help.

<details class="story-faq">
<summary>Can two people get different numbers?</summary>
<p>Yes. If one person counts each rise and each fall separately, their total can double. Practise together once while your pet is well. Use the same interval and compare your counts before interpreting a change. If the pet looks distressed, act on the effort rather than trying to reconcile the arithmetic.</p>
</details>
