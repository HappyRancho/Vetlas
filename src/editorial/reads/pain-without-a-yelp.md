A dog no longer runs upstairs when the door opens. A cat sleeps on a lower shelf. Changes like these are easy to explain away as age, laziness or preference. They can also be signs of pain. The AAHA pain guidelines emphasise recognising changes in function and reassessing response over time. Owners see activities that a clinician cannot reproduce in a short appointment.

## Start with a task the animal chose before
Choose one or two ordinary activities: stepping into a low tray, rising from bed, grooming the back or walking across a particular floor. Describe what used to happen, what happens now and when the change appeared. “Pauses, then takes the side step” gives more information than “looks sore.” Record good days too, along with medication timing and any change in activity.

A short, safely captured video can be valuable. Do not prod a painful area, force stairs or ask the animal to jump so the reaction can be filmed. Fear or excitement in a clinic can alter movement as well. Both the home and examination observations deserve a place in the assessment.

## Why a successful day can be misleading
An animal can run for a greeting and still have difficulty later. A floor with good grip may conceal a problem seen on tile. Conversely, new reluctance can have neurological, visual, environmental or other causes. Improved movement after treatment should be interpreted with the vet, alongside examination and any adverse effects.

## What counts as improvement?
Agree on an observable goal, such as getting up comfortably for breakfast or using the tray without hesitation. Ask when to review, how to report a setback and whether the home environment needs changing. A pet that suddenly cannot stand, collapses or has severe distress needs urgent assessment. Human painkillers are an unsafe shortcut to testing a theory.

## What to record after treatment begins

Pick the same activity and approximate time of day for follow-up observations. Note whether help was needed, how long the animal continued comfortably and whether a change lasted until the next scheduled treatment. Also mention appetite, vomiting, stool changes or unusual sleepiness when reporting the response to a medicine. The vet may need to separate a genuine gain in comfortable movement from sedation, excitement or a change in the daily routine. A calm, consistent record makes that distinction more possible.
