A dog limps after a walk. A human pain tablet is available, the veterinary clinic is closed and a fraction of the tablet looks small. That reasoning is dangerous. Dogs, cats and people process medicines differently. A household medicine can damage the stomach, kidneys, liver or blood even when the intention is to relieve pain.

## The package can mislead
A brand may sell several formulations. A cold-and-flu product can contain a pain reliever alongside other active ingredients. Two differently named products can contain the same drug. Paracetamol, also called acetaminophen, is particularly dangerous for cats. Human non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs can also cause serious harm in pets. Tablet size and a pet’s apparent size do not provide a safe conversion.

## When a pet already has prescribed pain relief
Adding a second product can introduce interactions, including risks when anti-inflammatory drugs and corticosteroids overlap. Even a medicine previously dispensed for this animal may no longer suit its hydration, kidney function or current treatment. Call the prescribing practice if pain breaks through. Tell them what the animal can no longer do, when the change began and when the last dose was given.

## If someone has already given a tablet
Contact a veterinarian or emergency service immediately. Provide the exact product, active ingredients, strength, maximum possible amount, time and body weight. Take the package. Do not wait for vomiting or other signs, and do not induce vomiting unless specifically directed. A pet that initially seems comfortable can still need early intervention.

For the home medicine cupboard, separate human and animal products and keep both in their original containers. Put the out-of-hours veterinary number where the person caring for the pet can find it. Removing the guesswork is a more reliable safeguard than hoping everyone remembers every unsafe drug.

## When the prescribed medicine seems to cause a problem

If a pet on a veterinary anti-inflammatory develops vomiting, diarrhoea, poor appetite or unusual lethargy, contact the prescribing team promptly and follow its directions before another dose. Give the last administration time and any other medicine, supplement or accidental human-tablet exposure. At the point of prescribing, ask for the product’s owner information and a clear contact plan. This is especially useful when an older pet is cared for by several people who might otherwise make different decisions about the same symptom.
