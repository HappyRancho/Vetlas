An owner who leaves with a specimen receipt instead of a new antibiotic may wonder whether treatment has been delayed. The first clinical question is whether bacteria are causing the problem at all. Inflammation, parasites, allergy and injury can produce overlapping signs. An antibiotic that helped during a previous episode does not identify the cause today.

## What the laboratory contributes
A culture attempts to grow organisms from a sample. Susceptibility testing assesses how an isolate responds to particular antimicrobials under laboratory conditions. The clinician still has to decide whether the organism explains the illness and whether an appropriate drug reaches the affected site safely. Sampling method matters. Contamination can lead to a convincing report about an organism that is incidental.

If a clinician recommends a sample before starting treatment, mention every medicine already given, including leftover drops or tablets. Previous exposure can change what grows. Ask what signs can safely be monitored while waiting and what would trigger immediate reassessment. A critically ill patient may need prompt treatment while results are pending.

## When the plan changes
A call to stop, narrow or change a medicine after results arrive can be a sign that new information has improved the decision. Confirm the drug, strength, interval and revised end date. Ask whether unused medicine needs returning or safe disposal. Do not share it with another animal or put it aside as a default response to the next flare.

The AAFP/AAHA stewardship guidelines place reassessment alongside prescribing. For owners, that has a concrete consequence: find out who will communicate the result, when to expect the call, and how to reach the team if the animal worsens before then. The follow-up conversation is part of treatment.

## An ear problem shows why the sample matters

For a dog with a recurring ear problem, the vet may first examine material under a microscope. That can show yeast, bacteria or inflammatory cells and may influence whether a culture will be useful. Repeating the last bottle simply because the smell seems familiar bypasses that step. Ask whether an underlying allergy, an obstructed canal or another issue is sustaining the problem. Even a well-chosen antimicrobial cannot by itself remove a foreign body or resolve every cause of repeated inflammation.
