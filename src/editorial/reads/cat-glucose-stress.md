The cat that hid under the waiting-room chair now has a blood result marked high. It is understandable to jump straight to diabetes. The result deserves attention, but a stressed cat can have a temporary rise in blood glucose. The pattern over time, and how the cat is living at home, change what can reasonably be concluded.

## Ask what else fits
A diabetic cat may drink and urinate more, lose weight and show appetite changes. A clinician looks for persistent hyperglycaemia and supporting findings, often including glucose in urine. A sample taken during an anxious visit is a snapshot. Tell the vet whether the cat has lost weight, whether litter clumps are larger and whether another animal shares the water or tray. These details affect how trustworthy the home history is.

## Why a different test can help
Fructosamine reflects average glucose exposure over a preceding period rather than a single minute of restraint. It can help distinguish sustained elevations from transient stress, but other conditions can affect interpretation. Repeat testing or carefully supervised home measurements may provide another view. Ask the clinician what result would resolve the uncertainty and how to collect it without making every measurement an ordeal.

## When waiting is unsafe
A cat with vomiting, weakness, refusal of food or marked lethargy needs prompt assessment, especially if diabetes is suspected or established. Ketones and systemic illness change the question from “Is this stress?” to “Does this cat need urgent stabilisation?” Do not start, borrow or increase insulin based on a single flagged result.

For an established patient, show the team the meter, units, meal and dose timing along with the numbers. A graph detached from its feeding and treatment history can be as misleading as a single result detached from the patient.

## What a home measurement cannot answer alone

Home testing can reduce one source of stress but introduces others. A poorly tolerated sampling attempt, unfamiliar device or reading taken at a different point in the meal and insulin cycle can change what the number means. Ask the team to observe your technique and mark the times it wants measurements. Report symptoms even when they disagree with the display. For a weak or disoriented cat, follow the emergency plan rather than continuing to collect numbers until they form a cleaner pattern.
