A laboratory printout makes every flagged value look equally urgent. Clinicians read it differently. They look at the animal first, then at the size and pattern of the changes, how the sample was collected, and what was happening when it was taken. A complete blood count describes cells circulating in the blood. It is only one part of a wider investigation.

## Which cell population changed?
Red-cell measurements help assess oxygen-carrying capacity and may suggest anaemia or the effect of dehydration. White-cell numbers and the distribution of cell types can change with inflammation, stress and other processes. A high white count does not, on its own, prove that bacteria are causing illness or that antibiotics will help. Platelets are important in clot formation. If a low machine count is unexpected, a clinician may examine a smear for clumps or other explanations.

## Is it one result or a pattern?
A small change at the edge of a laboratory’s reference interval is different from a rapid fall across several measurements. Reference intervals vary with species, method and laboratory. Keep the full reports, dates and units rather than recording only red or green arrows. A value measured after fluid therapy can also differ from one measured when a patient was dehydrated.

## What decision does it inform?
Ask whether an abnormality fits the signs, whether it changes what needs to happen today and which test or observation will help next. For example, an anaemic patient may need investigation into blood loss, destruction of cells or inadequate production. Simply adding iron at home can miss the underlying problem.

If the animal collapses, develops difficulty breathing or has significant bleeding, seek urgent assessment regardless of yesterday’s report. A laboratory result captures a moment. It should refine the clinical picture rather than freeze it in time.

## Ask for the report, not a photograph of the red arrows

A referral vet needs the animal’s identity, date, laboratory, reference intervals and units. A cropped phone image of the abnormal column removes much of that context. Ask for the whole report, including any comments about sample quality or manual examination, plus the reason blood was drawn. Note whether the animal was ill, recovering or attending a planned screening visit. A clinician may reasonably take different next steps for the same small abnormality in those three circumstances.
