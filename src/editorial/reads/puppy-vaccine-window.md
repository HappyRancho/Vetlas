A new owner is handed a puppy and told, “The first vaccination is done.” That is useful information, but it leaves three unanswered questions: which vaccine, at what age, and what documentation exists? Protection develops through an immune response. It does not appear merely because liquid was injected on a particular day.

## The puppy inherits an advantage and a complication
Early in life, antibodies acquired from the mother can help protect a puppy. Those antibodies decline at different rates between puppies. While present, they can also interfere with the immune response to some vaccines. There can be a period when there is too little maternal protection against infection yet enough antibody to interfere with vaccination. Owners cannot identify that window by appearance.

This is why WSAVA’s guidance for core puppy vaccination uses a series at age-appropriate intervals, with attention to the age at the final puppy dose. Timing, local disease pressure, vaccine type, the product label and the puppy’s health all matter. Counting clinic visits or injections without identifying their contents can give false reassurance.

## The risk question is local
Ask the practice about parvovirus risk around your home, safe forms of social exposure, and what to avoid until your puppy’s own plan is complete. Total isolation has costs for behaviour, but uncontrolled exposure to high-risk areas can have serious consequences. A vet who knows the local situation can help choose controlled experiences. Rabies vaccination also has public-health and legal requirements that need their own record.

## The useful record at handover
Take a clear photograph of each complete record page and ask the issuing clinic to fill gaps. A deworming date may sit beside a vaccine date on the same card. At the next visit ask what has been given, what remains and what the puppy can safely do before then. Write down a date rather than relying on “come back next month.”

## A useful question at the end of each visit

Ask the clinician to point to the component names on the label and explain which disease risks each addresses. A combination product can cover multiple diseases in one injection, while a different injection may address only one. That is why “three shots” can describe different histories. If a visit was missed, give the real dates and the age at administration. Let the vet choose the next dose from the documented history instead of trying to restart or complete a series on your own.
