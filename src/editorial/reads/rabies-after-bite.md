A neighbour offers to find the dog’s vaccine card. Someone else wants to check whether the scratch broke the skin. The injured person can already be doing something useful. WHO advises washing and flushing exposed wounds with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes and seeking prompt medical assessment. Serious bleeding or other major injury also needs emergency care.

## Why a small scratch deserves a clear history
Rabies virus can be transmitted through infected saliva entering broken skin or mucous membranes. A doctor needs to know the animal involved, whether skin was broken, whether saliva contacted an eye or mouth, and when and where it happened. A small mark or lack of obvious bleeding does not settle the exposure assessment. Do not apply chilli, caustic substances or home remedies.

## What the human clinic decides
Post-exposure prophylaxis can include a course of rabies vaccine and, for certain exposures, rabies immunoglobulin or an appropriate monoclonal antibody product. Previous human vaccination changes the recommended plan. Give the clinician any reliable records, but do not wait to find them before seeking care. Follow the schedule and the public-health team’s instructions about the animal. Watching a dog at home is not a substitute for that first assessment.

## Where the veterinarian fits
A vet can advise on the animal’s health, vaccination records and appropriate observation or reporting with local authorities. Do not try to capture an unfamiliar dog or handle wildlife to establish its status. If a pet was exposed as well, describe that exposure separately to its veterinarian. The human and animal have distinct clinical pathways.

The strongest everyday preparation is simple: keep the pet’s rabies record accessible, teach children to report even a scratch immediately, and know where human post-exposure care is available locally. A directory of veterinary practices serves the animal side of that plan.

## Two records that should stay separate

Keep the medical record for the exposed person and the veterinary record for the animal. For the person, note the date and location of the exposure, first aid, clinic attended and the next appointment. For the animal, retain its description, owner contact if known and any reliable vaccination document. If a vaccine card arrives later, pass it to the team overseeing the exposure. Do not independently cancel a scheduled human vaccine appointment on the basis of a photograph sent by a neighbour.
