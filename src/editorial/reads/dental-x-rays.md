A cat finishes its bowl, the visible teeth look clean, and the vet recommends dental radiographs. It can sound like a mismatch until you consider how little of a tooth you can see. The crown is exposed. The roots and the supporting bone are not. Cats can continue eating through painful dental disease, sometimes swallowing food with very little chewing.

## What changes once the team can see below the gum
A conscious oral examination can identify inflammation, broken crowns, swelling and visible deposits. It cannot map every periodontal pocket or reliably show what is happening along a root. Under anaesthesia, the team can probe, chart and obtain intraoral images. These findings can change a treatment plan that was necessarily provisional when you first discussed the estimate.

Tooth resorption in cats is a useful example. Part of the tooth is being lost, and what appears at the surface may tell only part of the story. Radiographs help the clinician evaluate the root and choose a treatment approach. The decision is based on the particular tooth, the images and the examination.

## Why scraping the surface answers a different question
Removing visible material may make the crown look better. It cannot establish the health of supporting structures or treat disease beneath the gum. The AAHA dental guidelines describe a comprehensive procedure that includes assessment and management of pain as well as cleaning. Ask the practice what its proposed procedure includes, how it manages anaesthesia and when the team will call if the findings change the plan.

## What to ask at discharge
Request a dental chart or a plain-language list of teeth treated and why. Ask when to resume chewing and brushing, what food texture is appropriate and which signs need a call. That converts a technical set of images into something observable at home: comfortable eating, healing gum tissue and a prevention plan suited to the remaining teeth.

## Reading the estimate before the procedure

An estimate prepared during a conscious examination may include a range because the full extent of disease becomes apparent under anaesthesia. Agree whether the clinician should telephone if imaging reveals an unexpected tooth that needs treatment. Leave a number you can answer and ask how pain will be managed if treatment must be staged. At collection, ask the clinician to point to one example on the radiographs. Seeing the relationship between crown, root and surrounding bone makes the explanation easier to remember when the pet returns for another check.
